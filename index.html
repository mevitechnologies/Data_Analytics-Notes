Chapter 1 · The Basics

What is Data?

Before learning analysis, we must understand the thing we are analysing.

Data is any recorded fact, number, observation, response, measurement, or piece of information that can be stored and examined.

Data is created whenever an event is recorded. When a customer buys a product, the order creates data. When a student receives a mark, the mark becomes data. When a fitness tracker records steps, that measurement becomes data.

Data does not have to be a large spreadsheet. It can be a number, sentence, date, category, image, yes/no answer, location, or another recorded observation.

Simple way to remember it Data is the raw material. Analysis is the process used to turn that raw material into understanding.

Chapter 2 · Meaning

What is Data Analysis?

Data analysis is the process of cleaning, exploring, interpreting and communicating data to find useful information that supports better decisions.

Imagine a doctor receiving a patient with symptoms. The doctor does not immediately prescribe a treatment. The doctor collects information, checks it, performs tests, looks for patterns, reaches a diagnosis, and then decides what action is appropriate.

Data analysis follows a similar logic: raw data → checking → exploration → evidence → interpretation → decision.

The analysis journey

1
Raw Data

Messy records are collected from a source.

2
Cleaning

Errors, gaps, duplicates and invalid values are checked.

3
Exploration

Questions are asked and patterns are investigated.

4
Insight

The analyst explains what the pattern means.

5
Decision

The organisation decides what to do next.

Five Questions

Types of Data Analytics

The presentation uses five practical questions that move from understanding the past to considering future action.

01

Descriptive

What happened?

Descriptive analysis summarizes historical data. For example: “Sales dropped 12% in March.”

It tells us what has already happened.
02

Diagnostic

Why did it happen?

Diagnostic analysis investigates possible reasons behind an observed result. For example: “Late deliveries in Pune were associated with cancellations.”

It tells us what may explain the result.
03

Monitoring

What is happening now?

Monitoring uses current or frequently refreshed data to track performance. For example: “342 orders have been recorded today.”

It tells us what is happening currently.
04

Predictive

What could happen next?

Predictive analysis uses historical patterns and models to estimate future outcomes. For example: “Jacket demand may rise in November.”

A prediction is an estimate, not a guarantee.
05

Prescriptive

What decision should we consider?

Prescriptive analysis connects evidence to possible actions. For example: “Increase jacket stock before the seasonal demand period.”

The goal is to connect evidence with action.

Chapter 2 · Data Fundamentals

Qualitative & Quantitative Data — The Foundation of Data Analytics

Before calculating an average, creating a chart, cleaning a column, or applying a statistical method, a data analyst must understand what each variable actually represents. This is the foundation of correct analysis.

A column containing numbers is not automatically quantitative, and a column containing words is not automatically qualitative. The meaning of the variable is more important than the appearance of its values.

The Core Data Classification
Start with the two broad families: Qualitative and Quantitative. Then refine them into Nominal / Ordinal and Discrete / Continuous.

Part A · Qualitative Data

What is Qualitative Data?

Qualitative data describes qualities, characteristics, labels, groups or categories.

Qualitative data helps answer questions such as “Which category?”, “What type?”, “What group?” and “What level of response?”

Examples include city, product category, payment method, department, colour, customer segment, satisfaction level and service priority.

Qualitative values are often words, but they can also be stored as numbers. For example, a survey may store 1 = Poor, 2 = Fair, 3 = Good, 4 = Very Good, 5 = Excellent. Those numbers are codes for ordered categories; their storage format does not by itself determine the conceptual data type.

Core idea: Qualitative data primarily tells us what category or quality an observation belongs to.
Think in Categories

What can qualitative data tell us?

  • Which city has the most orders?
  • Which payment method is most common?
  • Which product category is popular?
  • How many customers selected “Excellent”?
  • What percentage of orders were returned?
Bengaluru UPI Jacket Excellent

Qualitative Data → Two Core Types

Qualitative Type 1

Nominal Data

Categories with no meaningful natural order.

Nominal data mainly acts as names or labels. We can count how often each category occurs, but one category is not meaningfully “greater” or “smaller” than another.

For example, Bengaluru, Mumbai and Delhi are city categories. If a database stores them as 1, 2 and 3, the numbers are only codes.

Common mistake: 1 = UPI, 2 = Card, 3 = Cash does not make payment method quantitative. The numbers are labels.
Qualitative Type 2

Ordinal Data

Categories with a meaningful order or ranking.

Ordinal data allows us to say that one category is above or below another according to a meaningful order. However, the exact distance between categories is not necessarily equal.

Example: Poor → Fair → Good → Very Good → Excellent. The order is meaningful, but the gap from Poor to Fair is not automatically the same numerical distance as the gap from Very Good to Excellent.

Memory: Nominal = name. Ordinal = order.

Part B · Quantitative Data

What is Quantitative Data?

Quantitative data represents numerical quantities that can be counted or measured.

Quantitative data answers questions such as “How many?”, “How much?”, “How long?”, “How far?”, “How heavy?” and “How large?”

Examples include quantity sold, number of students, price, revenue, age, height, weight, temperature, delivery time and distance.

Numerical operations such as totals, averages, differences and measures of spread can often be meaningful for quantitative variables, but the correct operation still depends on the variable's exact meaning and measurement scale.

Core idea: Quantitative data tells us how much or how many.
Think in Quantities

What can quantitative data tell us?

  • How many products were sold?
  • What is the average order value?
  • How much revenue was generated?
  • How long did delivery take?
  • How much did temperature change?

Quantitative Data → Two Core Types

Quantitative Type 1

Discrete Data

Numerical values that are naturally countable.

Discrete values usually arise when we count separate objects or events. The values are distinct, and fractional parts of the counted object are generally not meaningful.

Examples: 60 students, 1,250 orders, 17 complaints and 8 products.

Memory question: Can I naturally ask “How many?” and get a count? Think Discrete.
Quantitative Type 2

Continuous Data

Numerical values obtained mainly by measurement and capable of varying across a range.

Continuous measurements can take decimal values depending on measurement precision. Between two values there can potentially be many additional values.

Examples: 172.6 cm height, 68.45 kg weight, 28.7°C temperature and 34.52 minutes delivery time.

Memory question: Am I measuring how much, how long, how far or how heavy? Think Continuous.

Tricky Examples — What Beginners Commonly Misclassify

Rating 1–5

If the values represent ordered satisfaction levels, the variable can be treated as an ordinal response. The numbers encode order.

Wrong shortcut

“It contains numbers, therefore it is quantitative.” Numbers can be codes, IDs, ranks or measurements.

Customer_ID = 10425

This is an identifier. The digits identify a record; they do not represent a measured quantity.

Quantity = 5

This is a count, so it is naturally discrete quantitative data.

Weight = 5.25 kg

This is a measurement, so it is naturally continuous quantitative data.

Payment code = 1, 2, 3

If the codes represent UPI, Card and Cash, the variable is nominal, not quantitative.

Other Important Data Classifications

The four core types describe the nature of a variable. Other classifications describe data from different perspectives and can coexist with them.

Structured

Rows and columns with a predefined schema.

Semi-Structured

Flexible structure with keys, tags or markers.

Chapter 3 · Data Structure

Structured, Semi-Structured and Unstructured Data

Data can also be classified according to how neatly it is organised and how easily a computer can interpret its structure.

Structured Data

Structured data follows a predefined structure, usually with rows, columns, fields and consistent data types.

Examples: SQL tables, Excel sheets, CSV files and transaction databases.

Easy to picture: a clean table with clearly defined columns.

Semi-Structured Data

Semi-structured data does not follow one rigid table format but contains tags, keys, metadata or other markers that provide organisation.

Examples: JSON, XML, many API responses and event records.

Think: flexible structure with identifiable fields.

Unstructured Data

Unstructured data does not naturally fit into a fixed row-and-column format.

Examples: images, videos, audio recordings, documents, free-form text and social media content.

Think: rich information that may require specialized processing.

Chapter 4 · Data Sources

Where Does Data Come From?

Data analysis begins with data collection. Understanding the source helps an analyst understand the reliability, context, limitations and appropriate use of the data.

Primary Data

Primary data is collected directly for a specific purpose or study.

Examples: surveys, interviews, experiments, observations and feedback forms created for the current study.

Example: A company asks 1,000 customers to complete a survey about a new product.

Advantage: The collection process can be designed around the exact question.

Secondary Data

Secondary data was collected previously by another person, team or organisation and is reused for a new analysis.

Examples: public datasets, government statistics, published reports, historical company records and research datasets.

Example: An analyst uses historical sales records collected by the organisation to study seasonal demand.

Important: Always check the source, definitions, date range and collection method before using secondary data.

Chapter 5 · Data Generation

How Is Data Generated?

Data is continuously created whenever people, machines, software systems and digital services perform activities that can be recorded.

Key idea: Modern data is not generated only when a person manually enters a value. Many digital systems create records automatically as part of normal activity.

Human Activity

People generate data by filling forms, writing reviews, sending messages, making purchases and responding to surveys.

Example: A customer gives a product 4 stars and writes a review.

Software Events

Applications can automatically record clicks, logins, searches, page views, errors and other events.

Example: An online store records when a user adds an item to a cart.

Sensors & IoT

Devices continuously measure physical conditions and send observations to software systems.

Example: A factory sensor records machine temperature every second.

Location Systems

GPS-enabled devices and services can generate location-related records when appropriately collected and permitted.

Example: A delivery application records the location of a delivery vehicle.

Images & Video

Cameras and user uploads create visual data that can later be analysed manually or with computer vision.

Example: A quality-control camera captures product images on a production line.

Machines & AI Systems

Automated systems create logs, predictions, measurements, model outputs and operational records.

Example: A recommendation system records which recommendation was shown and whether it was clicked.

The growth of data is driven by the increasing number of connected devices, digital services, transactions, sensors, applications and automated systems producing information.

The term Big Data is commonly used when the scale, speed, diversity or other characteristics of data create challenges that traditional approaches may not handle efficiently.

Volume

How much data exists?

Variety

Chapter 9 · Data Quality

Good Data Is More Than “Lots of Data”

A very large dataset can still be poor for analysis if the values are inaccurate, incomplete, inconsistent or collected without the information needed to interpret them.

Accuracy

Does the recorded value correctly represent the real-world value?

Example: A product price should match the actual transaction price.

Completeness

Are important values missing?

Example: A dataset with no city information may not support location analysis.

Consistency

Are the same concepts represented consistently?

Example: “Bengaluru” and “Bangalore” may require investigation and standardisation depending on the context.

Validity

Does each value follow the expected rules?

Example: A rating field expected to be 1–5 should be checked for values outside that range.

Timeliness

Is the information recent enough for the question?

Example: Yesterday's inventory may be more useful than a five-year-old stock report for a current operational decision.

Uniqueness

Are duplicate records incorrectly repeated?

Example: The same order appearing twice may inflate revenue totals.

Golden rule: Before asking “What does the data tell me?”, first ask “Can I trust this data for the question I am asking?”

Chapter 4 · Process

The Complete Data Analysis Workflow

A strong project starts with a business question rather than immediately opening a charting library.

1

Business Problem

Question: Which products drive revenue?

The first step defines what the organisation actually needs to understand. A vague question creates a vague analysis.

2

Data Collection

Example: Export orders from the website and app.

Identify where the required information lives and collect the relevant records.

3

Data Understanding

Question: What does each column mean?

Before calculating anything, understand the rows, columns, units, data types and meaning of each field.

4

Data Cleaning

Check missing ratings, duplicates, invalid values, inconsistent text and incorrect data types.

5

Data Transformation

Create useful fields such as Month or Revenue when the raw data does not already contain them.

6

Exploratory Data Analysis

Explore the data by city, category, month, price, quantity, discount and other meaningful dimensions.

7

Data Visualization

Use suitable charts to make trends, comparisons, distributions and relationships easier to understand.

8

Insights

Translate a chart or statistical result into a clear sentence describing what the evidence shows.

9

Business Decision

Use the insight as evidence when considering an action, such as planning stock or advertising.

Important: the workflow is iterative

Real projects rarely move perfectly from Step 1 to Step 9. During EDA, you may discover a missing value problem. During visualization, you may discover an unusual value that needs investigation.

Do not blindly delete unusual data. In the StyleCart example, a quantity of 500 is investigated and kept because it represents a genuine bulk order.

Good analysis means checking whether a value is wrong, unusual but valid, or evidence of a special situation.

Step 2 · NumPy

NumPy: Fast Mathematics on Numbers

NumPy provides efficient numerical arrays and mathematical operations that form an important foundation for data work.

NumPy is a Python library designed for fast numerical computation using arrays.

A normal Python list can store numbers, but NumPy arrays are designed for numerical operations over many values at once. This makes operations such as averages, sums, scaling and mathematical transformations concise and efficient.

The important beginner concept is vectorized computation: apply an operation to an entire array instead of writing a loop for every element.

Data Cleaning

Why Data Cleaning Matters

Real-world data is often incomplete, inconsistent or incorrectly formatted. If the input is wrong, the final analysis can also be misleading.

ProblemTypical Pandas toolExplanation
Missing valuesisnull(), fillna(), dropna()Identify blanks and decide whether to fill, remove or investigate them.
Duplicatesduplicated(), drop_duplicates()Find repeated records and determine whether they are accidental duplicates.
Wrong typesastype()Convert values into appropriate numeric, date or categorical representations.
Misspellingsstr.strip(), replace()Make category names consistent, such as “bangalore” and “Bengaluru” when they represent the same category.
Invalid valuesbetween()Check whether values fall inside an expected range, such as ratings from 1 to 5.
Outliersdescribe(), boxplot()Investigate unusually high or low values before deciding what they mean.

Cleaning is not deleting

A common beginner mistake is to assume that an unusual value must be an error.

StyleCart example: quantity = 500 A quantity of 500 is unusual compared with ordinary orders, but the presentation explicitly treats it as a genuine bulk order and keeps it.

The correct question is not “Is this value unusual?” The better question is “Is this value incorrect, or is it unusual but valid?”

Chapter 5 · EDA

Exploratory Data Analysis

EDA is where the analyst investigates the dataset before drawing conclusions or building models.

Exploratory Data Analysis, or EDA, is the systematic investigation of a dataset to understand its structure, quality, distributions, relationships, unusual values and possible patterns.

Think of EDA as detective work. You begin with questions, inspect the evidence, test possible explanations, visualize important relationships, and document what you discover.

The key question is: “What story is hidden inside the data, and what evidence supports that story?”

Three levels of analysis

Univariate

Study one variable. Example: the distribution of order prices.

Bivariate

Study two variables. Example: discount versus quantity.

Multivariate

Study three or more variables together. Example: city × month × revenue.

Load dataset
